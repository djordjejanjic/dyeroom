Ask photographers why medium format looks smoother than 35 mm and you will get a dozen answers: bigger negative, better lenses, shallower depth of field, a different feel. They are all partly right. One of the causes is simple and measurable, and it is grain. This page shows it on a single photograph.

## The short answer

Grain is a property of the film, not of the frame. A given emulsion has grains of silver, or clouds of dye, of a given size, whatever the size of the frame you expose. If you make the same print size from a small frame and a large one, the small frame has to be enlarged much more. The grain is enlarged with it, so it looks bigger in the print. The large frame is enlarged less, so the same grain looks finer.

A bigger frame also records the same scene with more film. Fine detail takes up more grains, so less of it is lost to the film's own structure, and the picture looks cleaner and more finely drawn.

## The four formats

Dyeroom offers four frame sizes. All are real formats you could load into a camera.

- **35 mm.** The frame is 36 × 24 mm, and the format of most cameras of the last century.
- **6×4.5.** On 120 roll film, about 56 × 42 mm. About two and a half times the area of a 35 mm frame.
- **6×6.** On 120 film, about 56 × 56 mm, square. About three and a half times the area.
- **6×7.** On 120 film, about 56 × 70 mm. More than four times the area of a 35 mm frame.

The picture fills as much of the frame as its shape allows, as if you had framed it that way in the camera. A 4:3 photograph on a 35 mm frame uses 32 × 24 mm of the 36 × 24 mm frame, for example.

## What Dyeroom does when you change the format

The format menu changes one thing: how much film each pixel of your photograph covers. On a bigger frame a pixel covers more film, so the grain looks finer, and less detail is lost to the emulsion. Everything else, the film, the exposure, the development, the paper and the filter, stays the same.

That is the point of the comparison, and also its limit. In a real camera, changing format also changes the lens you need, the depth of field at a given angle of view and the light the film gets. None of that is modelled here: your photograph was taken with the lens and aperture you chose, and it is laid on to the format afterwards.

In Dyeroom, changing the format lights up the button that develops again. *Previous* then puts the two formats side by side under the same Colour and Tones adjustments, so you can compare them on the same frame.

## It works for both films

On Sunday 64, the grain is made of dye clouds in the three layers. The film's published granularity figure sets how many independent grains there are, and the colour grain is the statistics of how many are left after the first developer. On Monday 400 it is silver, and it grows coarser as the density goes up. Either way, grain is tied to the film's physical grains, so a bigger frame shows finer grain in both.

## What else decides how grainy a picture looks

Format is one factor among several.

- **The film.** A slow film is finer than a fast one. Compare [Sunday 64](/films/sunday-64) with [Monday 400](/films/monday-400): they are different films, and not just different colours.
- **Development and pushing.** Pushing a black-and-white film raises its contrast and makes grain more visible. Monday 400's *Rated at* control does this at 1600 and 3200.
- **Density.** In black-and-white film grain grows with density. The shadows of a negative and the highlights of a print are not equally grainy.
- **Print size.** Make the print small enough and you will not see any of this. Grain is a question of viewing distance and enlargement.
- **Scanning and screens.** Seen on a screen, a developed picture is already reduced. Zoom to 100 per cent to see the film.

## Where photographers disagree

Forum answers disagree about grain, and not only because of taste. The same emulsion in 35 mm and in 120 roll film is not always the same product. Developers and processes vary. Some people mean the grain they see on a scan; some mean on a print. And many mix up grain with sharpness, which is a separate thing. The four-up above holds everything fixed apart from the frame, which a forum thread cannot do. It is a model of the effect, not a measurement of any particular roll.
